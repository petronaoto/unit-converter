Required area 5.7047 in². Nearest API 526 orifice: P, at 6.38 in².

Nobody queries the letter in a design review. They query the C, the P_cf, and whether you were really in critical flow.

So here are two gas cases sized on the same card, minutes apart.

The example the tool ships: 8,000 lb/h, M 19, T 560 °R, P₁ 179.7 psia → 0.7144 in² → orifice H.
The published reference case: 53,500 lb/h, M 51, T 627 °R, P₁ 97.2 psia → 5.7047 in² → orifice P.

Eight times the area. Two different letters. And two of the three intermediates do not move at all:

C = 346.9764 — both
P_cf/P₁ = 0.5457 — both
P_cf = 98.067 vs 53.045 psia — the only one that moves

Not a coincidence, and it is the thing worth carrying. Both C and the critical pressure ratio are functions of k alone:

C = 520·√( k·(2/(k+1))^((k+1)/(k−1)) )
ratio = (2/(k+1))^(k/(k−1))

Same k = 1.3, same two numbers, whatever else you change. P_cf moves only because it is that ratio times P₁, and P₁ was the one pressure that differed.

So a reviewer asking "where did C come from?" is really asking "what k did you use?" — and if yours came from a stream composition at relieving temperature rather than a textbook 1.3, that is the answer they want.

Now the part that carries risk. Both cases report Critical Flow, and both are critical because P₂ sits below P_cf — 0 psig against 98.067 and 53.045 psia. Not because the tool defaults there. Raise P₂ past P_cf and the same card switches to the subcritical equation, where back-pressure stops dropping out and starts sizing the valve.

Open the case, push P₂ up until it crosses P_cf, and watch the branch flip. The fastest way to see where the equation switches — and the cheapest way to find your assumed back-pressure on the wrong side of it.
